Insurance & Storm Claims

Does Insurance Have to Match My Shingles in Ohio?

Homeowner guide · 7 min read

The short version: there is no single rule that applies to every Ohio homeowner. Whether a mismatch repair is acceptable depends on your specific policy's language — and the facts about whether a matching shingle actually exists. That second part is something you can document.

Why Matching Matters After a Storm

Wind or hail often damages part of a roof — a slope, a section, a few dozen shingles. The natural repair question is whether new shingles can blend into the old ones. Two things work against a clean match: shingles weather and change color with years of sun exposure, and manufacturers discontinue product lines over time.

What Your Policy Actually Says

Coverage for matching is governed by your policy's terms, and policies differ. Some address matching directly; many are silent, which leaves the question to interpretation. Ask your insurer — in writing — how your policy treats matching before agreeing to a repair scope. Decisions about coverage rest with your insurer and your policy.

The Part You Can Control: Documentation

Whatever the policy says, the factual question comes first: can a matching shingle be obtained? That is answerable. A sample of your shingle can be sent to an independent laboratory such as ITEL, which identifies the manufacturer and product and reports whether it is still in production. The result is a document — not an opinion — that you and your insurer can refer to.

If the product is discontinued

A lab report showing discontinuation changes the conversation from "we think it won't match" to "the product no longer exists." How your insurer responds still depends on your policy — but the discussion happens on documented facts.

If the product still exists

Weathering may still make a spot repair visible. Photos of the repair area next to a new shingle of the same line document the color difference plainly.

If your insurer approved only a partial repair

You can ask about a re-inspection or provide additional documentation. Keep the contractor's scope and the insurer's estimate side by side and compare them line by line.
